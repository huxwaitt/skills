# Classifying sources

Outlets named here were checked in November 2026, most against their own funding and testing disclosures.
The test in section 1 governs, not this list. Funding models change, so if a page you open discloses a
different funding model from a ruling here, classify from the disclosure; a slogan such as
"reader-supported" is not a disclosure. An outlet that isn't listed gets classified by the same test.

The tiers, as used everywhere in this skill:

| Tier | Meaning | May be used for |
|---|---|---|
| **clean** | Gains nothing from which product you buy and takes nothing from makers. Two kinds: collective user ratings on a platform the maker doesn't control; testers that buy their own units and live mainly on reader/member money. | Subjective claims and facts. Can carry a recommendation. |
| **mixed** | Does real first-hand testing or reporting but has an indirect money tie: affiliate commissions, advertising, review units loaned by makers. | Corroboration, measurements and other facts. Never the reason for a pick. |
| **conflicted** | Direct stake: makes or sells the product; paid, gifted or sponsored by the maker for the coverage; bought rankings, awards or seals; reviews given for free product or reward; commission-driven roundups with no first-hand testing. | Facts only. Never subjective information. |

If you can't establish who pays, treat the source as conflicted.

A tier says whether a source can be trusted. Among clean sources, weight follows who is doing the rating:

- **Dedicated communities first.** A site people visit in order to rate and discuss the category draws
  informed users who have tried many: RateYourMusic, IMDb, BoardGameGeek, and the equivalent for whatever
  is being researched. A large pool there is the best collective opinion available.
- **General retailer ratings last.** They come from whoever happened to buy at that store. They are the
  fallback when a category has no dedicated community or tester, and a good place to find failure patterns.
- **No single store defines the field.** Don't mark a candidate down for having no pool at a big retailer.
  Specialist and dealer-sold brands won't have one; judge them on what their own community and the testers
  say, and say when that is thin.

## 1. How to classify a source

Answer four questions, in order. The first answer that lands in conflicted settles it.

1. **Who made or sells this product?** If the source is the maker, the seller of that specific product, or
   a platform owned by either, it is conflicted for that product.
2. **Who pays the source, and does the payment depend on what gets recommended or bought?** Money tied to a
   specific maker (sponsorship, paid placement, licence fees for a badge, paid previews) means conflicted.
   Money earned on whatever the reader buys (affiliate links, general ads) is an indirect tie.
3. **How were the test units obtained?** Bought at retail is clean. Loaned by the maker, or supplied as an
   ordinary press copy the outlet would get whatever it wrote, is mixed. Kept as a gift of real value, or
   supplied in return for guaranteed or favourable coverage, is conflicted. Not stated: assume the maker
   supplied it, so mixed at best.
4. **Was there first-hand testing at all?** A ranking assembled from spec sheets and other people's reviews,
   paid for by commissions, is conflicted no matter how lab-like it looks.

Then weigh the remaining ties: an outlet that buys its own units and lives mainly on reader or member money
is clean even with some incidental affiliate links; one that tests first-hand but lives mainly on affiliate
commissions, advertising or loaned units is mixed.

Where the answers are:

- The outlet's about, how-we-test, ethics, editorial-standards and how-we-make-money pages. These are fine
  evidence about the outlet itself.
- On the page: "we may earn a commission", "affiliate links", "sponsored", "paid partnership", "#ad",
  "in partnership with", "provided by the manufacturer", "sent to us for review", "we received this free".
- On user reviews: badges such as "incentivized review", "Vine customer review of free product",
  "originally posted on [brand].com", "collected as part of a promotion", sweepstakes or sampling notes.
- No disclosure anywhere and no way to tell how the site earns money: conflicted.

## 2. Rulings on common cases

| Case | Tier | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Brand or manufacturer site, its product pages and press releases | conflicted | Sells the product. Its specs, dimensions, warranty terms are usable facts. |
| Reviews on a brand's own store | conflicted | The maker hosts, moderates and can curate them. |
| Customer reviews on a multi-brand retailer or open marketplace (Amazon, Walmart, Target, Best Buy, eBay), from its own buyers with no incentive tag | clean | The platform earns on any sale, not on which brand wins, and the maker doesn't control it. The lowest-weight clean evidence, because the raters are whoever bought there: the fallback when a category has no dedicated community or tester. Still apply section 4. |
| Retailer aggregate where most visible reviews are tagged as syndicated from the brand's site or incentivised, and can't be filtered out | conflicted | The maker collected most of the pool, so the score can't be used. Say it was discounted and why. |
| A clean platform's ratings or verdicts republished elsewhere (data mirrors, price trackers, news write-ups of a tester's results, resellers showing another retailer's reviews) | tier of the origin | The judgment was formed at the origin. Record it as "origin via relay" with the original's date; the relay's own opinions take the relay's tier. |
| The same retailer's own-brand products (e.g. Amazon Basics, REI Co-op) | conflicted | There the retailer is the maker and controls the platform. |
| Incentivised or seeded reviews (Amazon Vine, sampling programmes, "free product for honest review") | conflicted | Free product in exchange for the review. Filter these out of a count where the platform tags them. |
| Retailer buying guides and "staff picks" | conflicted | Written by the seller to sell. |
| Affiliate "best of" roundups with no evidence of hands-on testing | conflicted | Commission-driven, nothing first-hand. Most "best X of 2026" listicles fall here. |
| Lab tester that buys its own units but lives mainly on affiliate links or ads | mixed | Real testing, indirect tie. Its measurements are among the best facts available. |
| Member-funded testing organisation that buys anonymously | clean | Even with incidental affiliate links or post-test logo licensing, as long as the test result can't be bought. |
| Tester that tests first-hand but keeps valuable units sent free by the maker | conflicted | A gift from the maker for the coverage; testing doesn't undo that. |
| Newspaper or magazine recommendation arm | mixed | Usually affiliate-funded with genuine testing; check whether units are bought or sampled. |
| Video reviewer who buys units, funded by platform ads, affiliate links or general sponsors | mixed | Indirect ties only. |
| Video reviewer with a maker sponsorship, paid preview, or "sent by the brand" for that product | conflicted | Paid or gifted by the maker for that coverage. |
| Dedicated enthusiast communities: category ratings databases, enthusiast forums, specialist subreddits | clean | Informed users with no maker money, many of whom have owned several products. A large rating pool, or a broad and consistent consensus among many independent owners across threads, can carry a pick. A single thread or a handful of voices is colour. |
| Forum posts by dealers, brand reps or accounts that only ever praise one brand | conflicted | Commercial stake posing as a neutral voice. Signs: new account, one brand, links to a shop, same phrasing across threads. |
| Platform where the reviewed business is the paying customer (Trustpilot, G2, Capterra) | conflicted | The business pays for the tools that choose who is invited to review, and vendor-funded gift-card campaigns are common. |
| Bought or licensed awards and seals (paid entry, pay-to-display badges, ad-linked seals) | conflicted | The maker pays the awarding body. A badge on packaging is never evidence. |
| Influencer posts, "honest review" from a creator in a brand's programme | conflicted | Paid, gifted or commission-tied to that brand; undisclosed ties are common, so assume one. |
| Crowdfunding pages and backer updates | conflicted | Written by the maker. Backer comments after delivery are colour. |
| AI-written or SEO filler (no named author, no photos of the unit, generic pros and cons) | conflicted | Can't establish who pays or whether anyone tested anything. |
| Government safety and recall databases | not tiered: facts | Recalls, complaint counts and crash or safety test results are facts, usable with any tier. A search that turns up no recall is enough; report "none found". |
| Owner surveys run by a member-funded organisation | clean | Large samples of real owners across many brands, nobody paid by the maker. They put brands on one scale and cover years of ownership, which star ratings mostly don't. |
| Critic score aggregates built from publisher-supplied review copies | mixed | Critics are usually given the product by its maker or publisher. |

## 3. Where collective opinion lives

The table below is a set of starting points and worked examples, not the list of allowed sources. Expect to
find better ones for the category in front of you, and classify each with section 1. Ways to find them:

- look first for the dedicated site where informed users rate this category: a ratings database, an
  enthusiast forum, a specialist subreddit (most hobbies and many product categories have one);
- in owner and trade forums, count independent owners and check who is a dealer, since dealers are
  conflicted;
- search for the category plus "owner survey", "reliability survey", "independent test" or "how we test";
- check testers in other countries when the same model is sold there;
- follow citations: a news write-up that quotes a survey or test names the source worth finding.

Clean sources carry the pick, a dedicated community ahead of a general retailer; mixed ones corroborate and
supply measurements. Notes give the one-line reason, so the same reasoning applies to outlets not listed.

are usually the best evidence there is, and often reachable only second-hand. Add an owner forum or
subreddit for failure themes and a member-funded tester's report if one exists; say plainly if none does.

## 4. Reading a rating properly

- **Count beside score, always.** 4.7 from 30 ratings is anecdote; 4.3 from 4,000 is evidence. Bands: under
  ~50 anecdote, ~50–500 medium, 500+ large; niche categories run smaller, so adjust and say so.
- **A count is not a score.** It tells you whether a score is noise, not which product is better. Once
  two pools are both large, compare the scores and ignore the counts; a tenth or two between them is a tie.
- **More than one pool.** Gather a candidate's ratings from every platform it is sold on. Agreement across
  platforms is stronger than one big number, and a product one store doesn't stock still gets a fair reading.
- **Distribution over average.** A J-shape (many 5s, a cluster of 1s) often means a reliability problem
  or a defective batch; read the 1s. A smooth hump around 4 is a product that's fine, not loved.
- **Recent vs. lifetime.** Compare the last 6–12 months with the lifetime score. A drop can mean a silent
  revision, cheaper parts or new firmware; a rise can mean a fix, or a review campaign.
- **Read the low-star reviews for repeated themes.** One angry review is noise; the same failure at the
  same age across ten reviews is a finding. Note the failure and when it occurs.
- **Signs of manipulation:**
  - merged listings, where reviews of an old or different product sit under a new one (check what the
    reviews actually describe);
  - review bursts, where many 5-star reviews arrive in a few days, especially near launch;
  - incentivised tags, "received free", Vine or promotion badges, and reviews mentioning a gift card or
    rebate for reviewing;
  - reviewer profiles with one review, or many 5-star reviews for one brand;
  - near-identical wording, or praise that names the product in full every time.
- **Same product, same thing.** Make sure the rating is for the exact model and version you're
  recommending, not a predecessor, a bundle, or a regional variant. A tester's verdict on one variant
  carries to a sibling that shares the body and motor; say which was tested and what differs.
- **One pool, counted once.** The same reviews shown on several country domains, mirrors or relays are one
  source, not several that agree.
